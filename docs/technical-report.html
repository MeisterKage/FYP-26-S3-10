1Executive summary

This report consolidates the project's requirements and design documentation into one current record, and describes the working prototype built against it.

The project builds Wollongong News, a news publishing platform that attaches a Credibility Score to every article and an Accuracy Rate to every contributor. Both are computed by a fixed arithmetic formula whose inputs are published, so that a reader can trace why one article scores differently from another instead of being asked to trust a ranking they cannot inspect.

Market analysis of six comparable products established the gap the project occupies. Existing services either rate whole publications without publishing them, or publish independent writers without assessing them. None scores the individual article, ties that score to the writer's own record, and shows the reader the arithmetic.

The prototype is deployed and operational. It comprises a React single-page application against a Flask REST API of 77 endpoints, backed by a PostgreSQL database of 23 tables hosted on Supabase, serving six user roles across 16 screens. The scoring engine is implemented as pure functions with no database access, no clock and no randomness, so any score can be recomputed and verified from its inputs alone. 139 automated test cases cover the engine, the permission middleware, the editorial workflow, error handling and rate limiting.

Section 7 records where the built system deviates from the PRD and PDD, and why. Three of those deviations require amendments to the design documentation rather than changes to the code.

2Introduction

2.1The problem

Publishing is now effectively free, and the volume of material presented as news exceeds any reader's capacity to verify it. The traditional signal of reliability, the masthead, has weakened as independent writers publish directly to audiences without an institution behind them.

This leaves a reader with a specific difficulty: deciding, in seconds, whether to trust an article written by someone they have never heard of. That situation is the one this project addresses. The reader is not asking whether the claim is true, which no software can settle for them. They are asking whether the writer has a record worth trusting and whether this particular article was carefully made.

Two responses dominate. Algorithmic feeds rank material by engagement and do not explain the ranking. Open publishing platforms apply no assessment at all. Both leave the reader without a usable signal, and the first actively conceals the one it has.

2.2How readers judge credibility today

Research into media literacy identifies the heuristics readers actually apply, each of which is unreliable on its own:

  • Brand recognition. Trusting a familiar masthead. Fails for independent writers and is exploited by imitation domains.
  • Presentation quality. Inferring rigour from typography and layout. Trivially imitated.
  • Corroboration. Checking whether other outlets report the same thing. Effective but slow, and defeated by syndication of a single wire story.
  • Sourcing. Checking whether claims are attributed and linked. Reliable, but readers rarely follow links.
  • Track record. Knowing whether the writer has been wrong before. The strongest signal available, and the one no platform currently surfaces.

The last two are the only heuristics a system can support without judging the truth of a claim itself. They are the inputs this project measures.

2.3The data a scoring system handles

Three kinds of data are held, and they behave differently.

DataContentsBehaviour
Master data Accounts and roles, content categories, editor assignments, and the platform scoring thresholds. Changes rarely, but a change to the thresholds rewrites every published score, so it is audited.
Editorial data Articles and their revisions, tagged sources, corrections, retractions, disputes and review decisions. Append-mostly. A correction is never deleted, because a corrected article is the record of that correction.
Engagement data Comments, likes, follows, saved articles, reader reports, subscriptions and payouts. High volume and mutable. Only upheld reports affect a score; the rest never touch it.

Table 2.1: Data classes and their handling characteristics.

The distinction matters to the design. Engagement data is the largest by volume and the least trustworthy as a signal, so it is deliberately excluded from the score except through the single, editorially mediated path of an upheld report. Popularity does not raise a credibility score on this platform.

2.4Target stakeholders

  • Readers who want a defensible reason to trust or discount an article before committing time to it.
  • Independent writers who have a record of accuracy but no masthead to vouch for them, and who currently cannot convert that record into readership.
  • Editors who need reader reports triaged so that attention goes where it is most needed.
  • Platform operators who must be able to defend a score to a writer who disputes it, which requires that the score be explainable.

2.5Project scope

The system covers the editorial lifecycle end to end: drafting, source tagging, submission, category-scoped editorial review, scheduling, publication, correction, retraction and appeal, together with reader engagement, reader reporting, premium subscriptions and a tiered contributor revenue share.

Six user tiers hold distinct permissions, described in §5.3.1.

Explicitly out of scope for version 1

  • Machine-learned credibility prediction, for the reason given in §4.2.
  • Semantic or predictive search. Ranking must stay inspectable.
  • Image and video analysis.
  • Automated fact-checking of claims.
  • Multi-language publishing and native mobile applications.
The bounding rule

If a reader cannot be shown why a score is what it is, the feature that produced it does not belong in version 1. Every exclusion above follows from that single rule rather than from schedule pressure.

3Research analysis

3.1Existing products

3.1.1NewsGuard

Assigns trust ratings to news websites against nine weighted journalistic criteria, assessed by trained journalists rather than by algorithm. Ratings are delivered through a browser extension that annotates search results and social feeds.

Strengths. The criteria are published, and human assessment catches nuance an algorithm misses. Limitations. The unit of assessment is the whole publication, so a weak article in a strong outlet inherits the outlet's rating and vice versa. Manual assessment does not scale to independent writers, and the extension is a paid product outside the reading experience.

3.1.2Ground News

Aggregates coverage of a single story across many outlets and displays the political distribution of that coverage, together with a blindspot feature showing which stories one side is not covering.

Strengths. Makes selection bias visible, which no other product does as well. Limitations. Measures political lean, not accuracy; a story can be balanced across the spectrum and wrong throughout. Depends on outlet-level bias ratings sourced from third parties.

3.1.3Media Bias / Fact Check

A manually maintained directory rating thousands of sources for political bias and factual reporting, with a written justification for each entry.

Strengths. Broad coverage, and each rating carries an explanation. Limitations. A reference directory with no publishing function; updates lag; and the methodology, while described, is applied by a small team whose judgement is not itself auditable.

3.1.4Full Fact

A UK charity that fact-checks individual claims made in public life, with automated claim-detection tooling that flags repeated claims for human checking.

Strengths. Operates at claim level, the finest useful grain, and publishes its workings. Limitations. Checks claims after they have been published elsewhere; it is a corrective rather than a publishing platform, and cannot scale to every article.

3.1.5Substack

Independent publishing with paid subscriptions and a straightforward revenue share, giving writers a direct relationship with readers.

Strengths. Proven economics for independent writers; the subscription model this project adopts. Limitations. No accuracy or credibility signal of any kind. A reader's only evidence is subscriber count, which measures popularity.

3.1.6Medium

Open publishing with editorial curation, publications, and a partner programme paying writers from member subscriptions.

Strengths. Low barrier to publishing and a mature reading experience: the design reference this project's interface is based on. Limitations. Curation is discretionary and its criteria are not disclosed, so a reader cannot tell why one article was distributed and another was not.

3.3Approaches to scoring

Four approaches were considered before selecting one.

ApproachStrengthsWeaknesses
Expert editorial rating
NewsGuard, MBFC		 Catches nuance; defensible in a dispute; criteria can be published. Does not scale; costly; the assessor's judgement is itself unauditable.
Crowd-sourced rating
Community Notes		 Scales at no marginal cost; incorporates distributed knowledge. Vulnerable to brigading; measures agreement rather than accuracy.
Machine-learned prediction Scales; can incorporate signals a human would miss. Cannot explain a specific score; inherits training-set bias; reproduces the opaque ranking the project exists to reject.
Disclosed formula
selected		 Every score decomposes into named contributions; reproducible from inputs; a writer can be told exactly why their score fell. Only measures what is countable; can be gamed at the margin, e.g. by padding source links.

Table 3.2: Scoring approaches evaluated.

The disclosed formula was selected. Its weaknesses are mitigated rather than eliminated: editorial review checks that tagged sources genuinely support the article, so padding is caught before publication, and an upheld report after publication costs considerably more than the sources gained.

3.4Necessary functionality

Research established a minimum set of functions such a platform requires:

  1. A publishing workflow with a review step, since a score assigned after unrestricted publication cannot prevent harm.
  2. A structured way to attach sources to claims, so that sourcing can be counted rather than inferred.
  3. A permanent, public correction record: a platform that silently edits errors has no track record to measure.
  4. A reader reporting channel, triaged editorially rather than acted on by count alone.
  5. An appeal route, because a score that cannot be challenged is not accountable.
  6. Role separation, so that no individual writes, approves and scores their own work.
  7. Runtime-configurable thresholds, so the platform can be tuned without redeployment.
  8. A complete audit trail of privileged actions.

3.5How such systems are built

Contemporary publishing platforms are built as a client application against a REST or GraphQL API, with a relational database for editorial data. The pattern the project follows places the API as the sole authority: no client reaches the database directly, so permission logic cannot be bypassed by a modified client.

Managed database services (Supabase, Neon, PlanetScale) have displaced self-hosted instances for projects of this size, providing connection pooling, automated backup and row-level security without operational overhead. Platform services such as Render and Railway deploy directly from a repository, which suits a student team without dedicated infrastructure.

3.6Recent developments

  • Provenance standards. The C2PA specification attaches cryptographically signed provenance to media files. It addresses whether an image was altered, which is complementary to, not a substitute for, whether an article is accurate.
  • Crowd-sourced annotation at scale. Community Notes demonstrated that bridging algorithms, which surface notes rated helpful across normally disagreeing groups, can moderate brigading, though the mechanism itself is not simple to explain to the reader.
  • Regulatory pressure. The EU Digital Services Act requires large platforms to explain recommender systems, which pushes the industry toward the explainability this project adopts by choice.
  • Generated content. The falling cost of producing plausible text raises the value of a per-author track record, since fluency is no longer evidence of care.

4.2Why arithmetic, not machine learning

A classifier trained on labelled examples would score more inputs than the formula does and would likely correlate better with expert judgement. It was rejected for three reasons.

  1. It cannot answer the question the product exists to answer. A reader asking why an article scores 62 needs a decomposition. Post-hoc attribution methods approximate this but are neither exact nor stable, and an approximate explanation of a score is precisely the hidden model the project rejects.
  2. It cannot be defended in a dispute. When a contributor appeals a score, an administrator must be able to state what changed and why. "The model weighted your article lower" is not a defensible position.
  3. There is no training data. Supervised credibility scoring requires a labelled corpus that does not exist for this platform, and borrowing one would import another organisation's editorial judgement unexamined.

The cost of the decision is accepted: the formula measures only what is countable, and ignores signals a human reviewer would notice. Editorial review is where those signals are caught, which is why review was made mandatory rather than optional.

4.3.3Security measures implemented

  • Argon2id password hashing with per-password salts, and enforced password strength rules stated before the field rather than after rejection.
  • Signed JWT sessions with expiry. A tampered token is rejected.
  • Row Level Security enabled on all 23 tables with default privileges revoked, so a leaked database key alone reads nothing.
  • HTML sanitisation on write using bleach, against a fixed tag allow-list. Injected scripts are stripped before storage, not escaped at render.
  • Rate limiting on authentication and write endpoints.
  • Security headers: X-Content-Type-Options, X-Frame-Options: DENY, a strict Content Security Policy, and Cache-Control: no-store on API responses.
  • Uniform responses on sign-in and password reset, so neither can enumerate accounts.
  • Parameterised queries throughout via the ORM; no string-built SQL.

5Proposed plan

5.1System development method

A single web application serves all six roles. There is no separate mobile build: the interface is responsive from 360 pixels upward, so a phone runs the same code as a desktop and no function is hidden at small widths.

ComponentPlatformAdvantagesDisadvantages
ClientReact 18 + Vite Component reuse across 16 screens; large community; fast rebuild during development. Client-side routing requires deliberate handling of deep links and browser history.
ServerFlask + SQLAlchemy Explicit routing and permissions; pure-function scoring engine is trivially testable. Synchronous by default; a long request blocks a worker.
DatabasePostgreSQL (Supabase) Relational integrity; RLS; managed backup; shared by the whole team. Network latency against a hosted instance; free tier pauses when idle.
HostingRender Deploys from the repository; one blueprint describes both services. Free tier sleeps after 15 minutes, giving roughly a 50-second first load.

5.2Software methodology

Both models are used, at different scales, because they answer different questions.

Waterfall governs the deliverables. The subject's milestones (requirements, design, prototype, final product) are fixed, sequential and externally dated. Planning against them is a waterfall exercise.

Scrum governs the development. Within each phase the work runs as short sprints against a backlog of user stories. This was not a stylistic choice: requirements did change during implementation, twice materially.

  • The database moved from MySQL to Supabase after the design document was signed off, which changed the deployment model.
  • The accuracy formula was rewritten after testing showed it penalised new contributors roughly eight times more heavily than established ones for the same error.

A strict waterfall would have carried both defects into the final product, because neither was visible until working code existed.

5.3Functional requirements

5.3.1User stories

Unregistered visitor
  1. As a visitor, I want to read published articles, so that I can evaluate the platform before registering.
  2. As a visitor, I want to see each article's credibility score and band, so that I can judge what to read.
  3. As a visitor, I want to see the breakdown behind a score, so that I can decide whether to trust the score itself.
  4. As a visitor, I want to browse by section and search by keyword, so that I can find relevant articles.
  5. As a visitor, I want to read how scoring works, so that I understand what the number means.
Free registered reader
  1. As a reader, I want to register and sign in, so that my activity persists across devices.
  2. As a reader, I want to comment and reply, so that I can discuss an article.
  3. As a reader, I want to like articles and follow writers, so that my front page reflects my interests.
  4. As a reader, I want to save articles to a reading list, so that I can return to them.
  5. As a reader, I want to report an inaccuracy with a category and an explanation, so that an editor can assess it.
  6. As a reader, I want to control which events email me, so that I am not over-notified.
  7. As a reader, I want to apply to become a contributor, so that I can write for the platform.
Premium reader
  1. As a premium reader, I want to open subscriber-only articles, so that I receive what I pay for.
  2. As a premium reader, I want to see what a subscription funds and how it is shared with writers, so that I can decide whether it is worth it.
  3. As a premium reader, I want to cancel and keep access until the period ends, so that cancelling is not punitive.
Verified contributor
  1. As a contributor, I want to draft an article privately, so that unfinished work is not visible.
  2. As a contributor, I want to format the body with headings, quotes and links, so that a long article is readable.
  3. As a contributor, I want to tag verifiable sources, so that my sourcing counts toward the article's credibility.
  4. As a contributor, I want to submit for review and be told the outcome with a reason, so that I can act on a rejection.
  5. As a contributor, I want to schedule publication, so that an article appears at the right moment.
  6. As a contributor, I want to see my accuracy rate and what moves it, so that I understand my own record.
  7. As a contributor, I want to see my subscriber count, revenue band and distance to the next band, so that I can plan.
  8. As a contributor, I want to dispute a correction I believe is wrong, so that my record can be defended.
Editor
  1. As an editor, I want a queue of submissions in my assigned sections, so that I review what is mine to review.
  2. As an editor, I want to read an article with its tagged sources listed, so that I can verify the sourcing.
  3. As an editor, I want to approve or reject with a reason, so that the writer can act on the decision.
  4. As an editor, I want to see flagged articles with every reader report in full, so that I decide on evidence and not on a count.
  5. As an editor, I want to uphold or dismiss each report individually, so that a single valid report is not lost among invalid ones.
  6. As an editor, I want to log a correction or retraction, so that the public record is accurate.
System administrator
  1. As an administrator, I want to see what is waiting on a decision, so that I know where to start.
  2. As an administrator, I want to search accounts and change roles, so that I can govern access.
  3. As an administrator, I want to suspend or delete an account, choosing whether its articles remain, so that removing a person does not silently remove the record.
  4. As an administrator, I want to decide contributor applications with the applicant's history in view, so that I judge on more than a writing sample.
  5. As an administrator, I want to assign editors to sections, so that review coverage is complete.
  6. As an administrator, I want to manage categories, so that the section structure can evolve.
  7. As an administrator, I want to adjust scoring thresholds and be told that doing so rewrites published scores, so that I am not surprised by the consequence.
  8. As an administrator, I want to resolve disputes with the correction, its score effect and the writer's case on one screen, so that I can judge fairly.
  9. As an administrator, I want an audit log of privileged actions, so that decisions are accountable.

5.3.6Database design

Twenty-three tables. The diagram shows the editorial core; engagement, notification and platform tables are listed beneath it.

Design decisions worth recording
  • Scores are stored, not computed on read. Each article holds its score and the full breakdown as JSON. A score must be reproducible as it stood, and recomputing on read would silently rewrite history whenever a threshold changed.
  • Accounts are soft-deleted. deleted_at preserves referential integrity for articles and corrections attributed to a removed account.
  • Corrections are never deleted, only overturned. An overturned correction remains visible with its outcome, because the appeal is itself part of the record.
  • One report per reader per article is enforced by a unique constraint, not by application logic, so a race between two requests cannot create two.
  • The dispute column is named argument, because case is a reserved SQL keyword: the ORM quotes it correctly but every hand-written query against it fails.

5.3.9The scoring engine

The engine is implemented as pure functions: no database access, no clock, no randomness. Given the same inputs it returns the same score, which is what makes a score defensible when a contributor disputes it.

Credibility score

An article's score is the sum of its positive components less its penalties, clamped to the range 0–100. The inputs are published; the tuning weights are internal and administrator-configurable, so no constant may be hard-coded into the interface.

ComponentBasisDirection
Contributor accuracyThe writer's smoothed accuracy rate, weightedRaises
Verifiable sourcesPoints per tagged source, subject to a capRaises
CorrectionsA penalty per logged correctionLowers
RetractionA single larger penalty if the article is retractedLowers
Upheld reader reportsA penalty once the report count crosses the administrator-set thresholdLowers

The score falls into one of four bands: High (80 and above), Moderate (60–79), Low (40–59) and Disputed (below 40). The band word is always rendered and is always the authority; no band is signalled by colour alone, so the reading survives greyscale printing and colour-blind vision.

Accuracy rate, and why it is smoothed

A contributor's accuracy rate was originally a plain ratio of clean articles to published articles. Testing against the seeded data showed that this was materially unfair.

ContributorArticlesCost of one correction, raw ratioCost, smoothed
New writer515.0 pts7.5 pts
Established writer401.9 pts1.7 pts
Ration/a7.9×4.4×

Table 5.3: Penalty for an identical error, before and after smoothing. Measured against the seeded dataset.

The rate is now blended toward a platform baseline, weighted by how much the contributor has published. A short record moves the figure cautiously; a long record moves it freely. This removed a second defect as well: under the raw ratio, publishing a fifth article could move a contributor's whole back catalogue by 22.5 points in a single step, because the rate crossed the threshold at which it began to be displayed.

A withheld rate is not a zero

Below a minimum article count, the profile reads New Contributor rather than showing a figure. The scoring engine still uses a smoothed value internally (the display is withheld, not the arithmetic), and the ledger marks the article as being by a new writer so a reader is told why no rate is shown.

8Case study

Trust in news media has declined across most surveyed markets over the past decade, while the volume of published material has risen sharply. The two trends compound: as more is published, less of it can be verified by any individual reader, and the absence of a usable signal is itself a reason to distrust everything.

The responses the industry has produced fall into two groups, and both have a structural weakness. Outlet-level ratings (NewsGuard, Media Bias/Fact Check) assess the publication. This is tractable, because there are thousands of outlets and millions of articles, but it assigns the same rating to the best and worst article a publication produces, and it has nothing to say about an independent writer with no publication behind them. Claim-level fact-checking (Full Fact, and the fact-checking programmes operated by the large platforms) is accurate but arrives after publication and cannot scale to everything.

The gap between them is the article, and the writer who produced it. That is the unit a reader actually encounters, and it is the unit no existing product scores.

This project's position is that the unit is scoreable without judging the truth of any claim, provided the system measures process rather than content. Whether a writer attributed their claims, whether they have been corrected before, and whether readers have raised concerns that editors upheld: these are all countable, and all of them correlate with care. None of them requires the platform to adjudicate what is true.

The approach has real limits, and it is worth stating them plainly. A careful writer who is wrong about something no one catches keeps a high score. A contentious but accurate article can attract reports that cost it credibility before an editor dismisses them. Sourcing can be padded at the margin. The formula measures the visible correlates of rigour, not rigour itself.

What it does provide is a signal that is legible. A reader who disagrees with a score can see exactly which input they disagree with, which is more than any algorithmic feed offers and more than an unexplained editorial judgement offers. A writer whose score falls can see why and can appeal it. In a domain where the failure mode of every existing system is opacity, an imperfect but inspectable measure is a defensible contribution.
